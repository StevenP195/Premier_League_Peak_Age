When Do Premier League Players Peak?

Steven Prusak

This report tracks 2,465 Premier League players across 149,637 individual match appearances and 14 seasons (2012/13 through 2025/26), combining Transfermarkt's match-by-match records with FBref's advanced stats and EA Sports FIFA ratings to ask a simple question: at what age does a footballer actually perform best? The answer depends heavily on what "best" means and what position a player fills — goal involvement, passing, defending, aerial duels, shot-stopping, and market value each peak at a different age, and none of them peak as early as most fans assume.

Finding 1 · Attacking output

Goal involvement peaks in a player's early 20s — unless they're a forward

Across every outfield position, goal involvements (goals + assists) per 90 minutes peak earliest for defenders, at age 20 (0.12 per 90), and for midfielders and forwards the smoothed curve keeps climbing into a player's mid-30s (Attack: age 34 at 0.52 per 90; Midfield: age 34 at 0.26 per 90). Goalkeepers are essentially flat near zero the whole way through (a peak of just 0.006 per 90 at age 32), which is expected — it's also why the rest of this report tracks keepers on shot-stopping instead of goals and assists.

Read the late peak for attackers and midfielders with one caveat: the Premier League only keeps its best older players on the pitch. A 34-year-old still starting matches has already survived a decade of squad cuts, so this curve mixes true aging with survivorship — it shows who stays productive at the top level, not what happens to the average player as they age.

Finding 2 · Passing

Passing accuracy peaks earlier than attacking output — around age 23

Pooling every outfield position with at least 450 minutes in a season and an FBref match, pass completion rate peaks at age 23 (77.7%), a full decade before the goal-involvement peak for attackers and midfielders. Passing is a more technique-driven skill that top-flight players arrive with already refined; it doesn't compound with the tactical experience and physical timing that late-career creative and finishing numbers seem to reward.

Finding 3 · Defensive work-rate

Defenders and midfielders do their most defending at 20, not 30

Tackles won plus interceptions per 90 minutes peaks at age 20 for both defenders (2.72 per 90) and midfielders (2.40 per 90), then trends downward through a player's late 20s and 30s. This is the opposite shape from the attacking curve in Finding 1: young defensive players entering the league appear to compensate for inexperience with sheer defensive activity and pressing intensity, while the players who keep starting into their 30s do it with positioning rather than volume of duels won.

Finding 4 · Aerial ability

Aerial duel success keeps rising all the way to age 35

Of every metric in this report, aerial duel win rate has the latest peak: 61.8% at age 35. Winning duels in the air rewards positioning, timing, and reading a cross — skills built on experience rather than raw athleticism — and the Premier League players still contesting headers at 35 are typically specialist centre-backs whose whole game is built around exactly that skill.

Finding 5 · Goalkeeping

Goalkeepers peak latest of all — shot-stopping tops out at 33

Save percentage among Premier League goalkeepers peaks at age 33 (65.9%), later than every outfield metric except aerial duels. Goalkeeping is the position where Finding 1's flat goal-involvement curve is completely uninformative on its own — shot-stopping is what actually separates a keeper's career arc, and it rewards the same experience-over-athleticism pattern seen in aerial duels and defending positioning.

Finding 6 · Market value

Transfer market value peaks years before most on-pitch metrics do — at 26

Average market value across all outfield positions peaks at age 26 (€14.1M), based on 4,235 valuation snapshots. Age 26 is also, separately, the single age that accounts for the largest share of all Premier League minutes played (11.76% of total minutes) — clubs pay the most for, and give the most minutes to, players who are past their defensive peak (Finding 3) but still years from their attacking, aerial, and goalkeeping peaks. The market is pricing potential and reliability together, not any single peak metric in isolation.

Finding 7 · Discipline

The "reckless youngster" stereotype doesn't hold — cards peak at 30

Yellow and red cards per 90 minutes are lowest at age 36 (0.138 per 90) but actually peak at age 30 (0.168 per 90), not in a player's early 20s as the stereotype of hot-headed youth might suggest. Players in their late 20s and early 30s — the same window where defensive positioning has replaced defensive volume (Finding 3) — appear to pick up more cards, plausibly from using fouls tactically to break up play rather than losing their composure.

Finding 8 · Playing time

No single age dominates minutes — the usage curve is broad and centered on 26

Even at its single most-used age, 26, that age group accounts for only 11.76% of all Premier League minutes ever played in this data set. The distribution across ages 20 through 33 is broad and gradual rather than sharply peaked, which matches the overall picture from this report: there is no one age at which "peak Premier League player" happens, because different skills top out anywhere from 20 (defensive work-rate) to 35 (aerial duels).

Finding 9 · Market value, name by name

Who was the most valuable Premier League player at each age?

Finding 6 showed the average market value curve; this is the individual record at each age instead — the single highest market value any player reached while contracted to a Premier League club, at that exact age. Erling Haaland owns the ceiling from 22 through 26, level at €200M in this data set, matching Finding 6's average peak age of 26 almost exactly. Kevin De Bruyne, a midfielder rather than a forward, holds the record at six different ages from 27 to 34, which is the longest individual run in the chart. The one real surprise is age 21: the record there belongs to Trent Alexander-Arnold (€110M), a right-back, in a chart otherwise dominated by attacking players.

Read the youngest ages carefully. The records at 17–20 all come from very recent (2025–2026) valuations of highly-rated academy players, not necessarily because those teenagers are generational talents on Haaland's level, but because transfer valuations across the whole market have risen substantially over the decade this data covers — a recent 18-year-old's record is competing against a 2010s 18-year-old's record set when valuations league-wide were lower. This chart shows single record-holders, not a smoothed trend, so unlike every earlier chart in this report it isn't filtered by minutes played or minimum sample size — that's intentional here.

About this data

Sources
Match-level appearances, player biographical data, and market valuations come from a Transfermarkt export (via Kaggle). Advanced per-match-derived stats (pass completion, take-on success, aerial duels, tackles, interceptions, goalkeeper save/clean-sheet rates) come from two FBref-sourced Kaggle datasets covering the 2017/18–2023/24 and 2024/25 seasons. FIFA/EA Sports FC player ratings (overall, potential, pace) come from a Kaggle mirror of the SoFIFA database, editions FIFA 15–23.
What one row is
The core data set is player-match: one row per player per Premier League match appearance (149,637 rows, 2012/13–2025/26). For the age-curve findings above, rows are aggregated to player-season (7,418 rows: one player, one season) and then averaged within one-year age bins, weighted by minutes played.
Rows dropped, and why
Player-seasons under 450 minutes (about five full matches) are excluded from every age-curve chart to keep small sample-size noise from a fringe player's cameo appearances from distorting the curve. Each one-year age bin is also required to have a minimum number of player-seasons behind it (15–30, depending on the metric) before it's plotted; thinner bins are dropped rather than shown. Players missing a position or date of birth in the source data are excluded entirely. Because none of the three sources share a player ID, the FBref and FIFA files are joined onto Transfermarkt records by normalized player name, which matched 62% of core player-seasons to FBref stats and 37% to FIFA ratings — unmatched seasons simply have blank values for those columns and are excluded from charts that depend on them.
How rates are computed
"Per 90" rates divide a summed total (goals, assists, tackles won + interceptions, cards) by total minutes played in that player-season, then multiply by 90. Percentage rates (pass completion, take-on success, aerial duel win rate, save percentage) are taken directly from FBref's own season-level percentages and averaged across players within an age bin, weighted by minutes played. Every age-bin line in the charts above is a 3-year centered moving average of the minutes-weighted per-age value, to smooth single-age sample noise without changing where each curve peaks by more than a year.
